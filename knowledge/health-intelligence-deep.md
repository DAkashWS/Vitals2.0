---
title: Reading the body in space, in depth
source: NASA, ESA and published studies, gathered by team Mission 61:4
---

## Why can a single reading be misleading?

One number is a snapshot. Heart rate jumps after climbing through a hatch, temperature changes through the day, and SpO2 dips with cold hands. Patterns over days, compared with your own baseline, tell you far more than any single reading.

## What is heart rate variability?

The tiny changes in time between heartbeats. Higher variability usually means the body is rested and relaxed; lower variability can come with stress, poor sleep, illness or overtraining. It's a useful trend to watch, not a number to judge on its own.

## How much does a fever raise your heart rate?

As a rough rule, resting heart rate goes up by around 10 beats per minute for every 1 °C of fever. So a raised heart rate together with a raised temperature points to the body fighting something, and both should be watched together.

## Does body temperature change during the day?

Yes. It's usually lowest in the early morning and highest in the late afternoon, by up to about 1 °C. In space, core temperature also tends to run higher than on Earth. That's why VITALS compares readings at the same time of day with your own baseline.

## Why can an SpO2 reading be wrong?

Cold fingers, moving, nail polish, bright light on the sensor or a loose clip can all give a low or jumpy reading. Before worrying, warm your hands, keep still, and measure again. If it stays low, check the cabin readings and tell the flight surgeon.

## Do astronauts grow taller in space?

Yes, by up to about 3 %, as much as 5 cm. Without gravity squeezing them, the discs in the spine expand. It's one reason back pain is so common in the first days, and why spacesuits are fitted with room to spare.

## What is space anaemia?

A 2022 study found that astronauts' bodies destroy about 54 % more red blood cells in space than on Earth, around 3 million a second instead of 2 million. The effect continued for the whole mission and for months after. It can add to tiredness and to feeling faint after landing.

## What happens to the immune system in space?

It shifts in several ways: some immune cells work less well, others become overactive. Viruses that sleep in the body, like the ones behind cold sores and shingles, wake up more often in astronauts, usually without symptoms. Good sleep, food and hygiene matter even more up there.

## What did the NASA Twins Study find?

Astronaut Scott Kelly spent nearly a year in space while his identical twin Mark stayed on Earth. Scott's telomeres (the caps on chromosomes) got longer in space and then shortened after landing, and thousands of genes changed activity. Most changes returned to normal within months.

## Does the heart change shape in space?

Yes. Studies found the heart becomes rounder, about 9 % more spherical, because it doesn't have to pump against gravity. It returns to its normal shape after landing. The heart also tends to get a little smaller if exercise isn't kept up.

## How do astronauts weigh themselves without gravity?

Scales don't work in weightlessness, so they measure mass by motion. A spring device pushes the astronaut back and forth, and the speed of the movement gives their mass. Weight loss is common in space, so it's checked regularly.

## How much do astronauts actually sleep in space?

Studies show many astronauts average around 6 hours a night, less than the 8.5 hours scheduled. Noise, light, excitement, work and the strange body position all play a part. Poor sleep shows up in slower reaction times, which VITALS tracks.

## Why do astronauts' fingernails sometimes fall off?

During spacewalks, the stiff, pressurised gloves press on the fingertips for hours. Some astronauts have had fingernails come loose afterwards. Glove fit, padding and keeping nails short help.

## Does skin change in space?

Skin can get thinner and more sensitive, and dry air causes itching and rashes. The hard skin on the soles of the feet softens and peels away because you never walk, while the tops of the feet get rough from hooking under foot rails.

## Which readings tend to change together?

A few common pairs: CO2 rising and headaches; poor sleep and slower reactions; low water intake and a higher heart rate; fever and a higher heart rate. When two readings move together, VITALS points it out, because one may help explain the other.

## Why does reaction time matter on a mission?

Docking spacecraft, driving the robotic arm and spacewalking all need quick, precise responses. Reaction time slows with sleep loss, stress and high CO2, often before you notice feeling tired. Short reaction tests are a simple early warning.

## What is space adaptation syndrome?

The nausea, disorientation and headaches that many people get in their first days in space, as the brain gets mixed signals from the eyes and inner ear. A large share of first-time flyers have some symptoms. It usually settles within a few days.

## How long does a message take to reach Mars?

Between about 3 and 22 minutes one way, depending on where Earth and Mars are in their orbits. So a question and answer takes up to three-quarters of an hour. That's why a Mars crew needs tools like VITALS that explain readings without waiting for Earth.

## What wearable sensors have been used on the space station?

The Canadian Space Agency's Astroskin shirt has measured heart rate, breathing, temperature, blood oxygen and activity on the ISS. Crews also wear actigraphy watches to track sleep and light. VITALS is designed to sit on top of sensors like these and explain what they show.

## Why is your own baseline so important in space?

Spaceflight shifts normal values: resting heart rate, temperature, blood volume and sleep all change. A reading that's normal for one astronaut can be a warning for another. Comparing with your own in-flight baseline catches real changes without false alarms.

## What does the gut microbiome do in space?

The bacteria in the gut change during spaceflight, influenced by the packaged diet, stress and the closed environment. Changes have mostly returned to normal after landing. Fresh food and a varied diet are thought to help keep it healthy.

## Why do astronauts get headaches so often?

Headaches are one of the most common complaints in space. CO2 in the air, fluid shifting to the head in the first days, poor sleep, dehydration and eye strain are the usual causes. Track them alongside cabin CO2 and sleep: that's where patterns appear.
