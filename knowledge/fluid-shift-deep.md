---
title: Fluid shift, in depth
source: NASA, ESA, Roscosmos and published studies, gathered by team Mission 61:4
---

## How quickly does fluid shift start after launch?

Within minutes to hours of reaching weightlessness. The face swells most in the first day or two, then settles a little as the body gets rid of some of the extra fluid. The shift itself lasts for the whole mission.

## Why do astronauts look younger in space?

The fluid moving into the face plumps out wrinkles and lines, so many astronauts look smoother and rounder in photos from orbit. The effect disappears within a day or so of landing, when gravity pulls the fluid back down.

## How much do the legs shrink in space?

Each leg can lose around 1 litre of fluid volume in the first days, so the legs look thinner, socks feel loose and the calves look slimmer. That's the "bird legs" half of puffy face, bird legs.

## Why do astronauts need special glasses in space?

Fluid shift can flatten the back of the eye, which tends to make vision more long-sighted. Astronauts fly with "space anticipation glasses" in several strengths, or adjustable lenses, so they can still read checklists and screens if their eyes change.

## What do eye doctors look for in astronauts?

Swelling of the optic disc (where the nerve leaves the eye), folds in the layers at the back of the eye, and a flattening of the eyeball. Crews take eye images and ultrasound scans in flight, guided by experts on the ground.

## Can ultrasound be done in space?

Yes. The station has an ultrasound machine, and astronauts with little training have scanned each other's eyes, hearts and neck veins while an expert on the ground talked them through it. It's how a blood clot in an astronaut's neck vein was found in 2020.

## What are Braslet cuffs?

Russian thigh cuffs that gently squeeze the tops of the legs, trapping some blood there for a while. That reduces the amount pooling in the head and can ease the puffy, stuffy feeling. They're used for limited periods, as part of a countermeasure plan.

## What is the Kentavr suit?

A Russian compression garment worn under the spacesuit during and after landing. It squeezes the legs and lower body so blood doesn't drop to the feet so suddenly when gravity returns, which helps prevent fainting.

## Can you sleep in a suction sleeping bag?

Researchers have tested sleeping bags that apply gentle suction to the lower body at night, to pull fluid back toward the legs for hours at a time. The idea is to protect the eyes on long missions without taking up the crew's working day.

## Why are men more likely to get eye changes and women more likely to faint after landing?

Studies have found that eye changes (SANS) show up more often in male astronauts, while women more often have trouble with fainting on standing after landing. The reasons aren't fully understood, which is why every astronaut is monitored, not just one group.

## How do scientists study fluid shift on Earth?

With head-down bed rest. Volunteers lie in bed tilted 6 degrees head-down for weeks or months, without ever sitting up. Fluid moves toward the head, much like in space, so new countermeasures can be tested before they fly.

## Why does food taste bland in space?

The stuffy, congested nose caused by fluid shift dulls the sense of smell, and taste depends heavily on smell. That's why many astronauts reach for hot sauce, shrimp cocktail with horseradish and other strong flavours in orbit.

## Why do astronauts pass more urine in the first days?

The body senses extra fluid in the chest and neck and reads it as too much blood volume. It responds by passing more urine and turning down thirst. Within days, blood volume has dropped by around 10–15 %.

## Why do you need to keep drinking even if you don't feel thirsty?

Because the body's thirst signal is turned down by the fluid shift. If you wait for thirst, you're already behind. Drinking on a schedule and checking urine colour are the simple, reliable ways to stay hydrated.

## How do astronauts drink in space?

From sealed pouches with a straw and a clamp, so water can't float away. Drinks are often powders mixed with recycled water. A floating ball of water is fun to watch but a hazard near electronics.

## What happens to the body after landing back on Earth?

Gravity pulls blood back down to the legs, but there's less of it than before. The heart and blood vessels have to relearn how to fight gravity. Many astronauts feel dizzy and heavy at first, so they're helped out of the capsule and given time before standing for long.

## How long does it take to readjust to gravity after a mission?

Balance and blood pressure control usually come back over days to weeks. Fluid and blood volume recover within days to weeks too, while eye changes in some astronauts can last much longer. Rehabilitation programmes after long flights run for around six weeks.

## Does fluid shift happen on the Moon or Mars?

Partly. The Moon has a sixth of Earth's gravity and Mars about 38 %, so fluid is pulled down less than on Earth. How much protection partial gravity gives is still unknown, and it's one of the big questions for future bases.

## Could artificial gravity stop fluid shift?

Spinning a spacecraft, or a short-arm centrifuge that spins an astronaut on a bed for part of the day, could pull fluid back toward the feet. Centrifuges have been tested on Earth and with animals in space, but no crewed spacecraft spins for gravity yet.

## Why do neck veins look full in space?

On Earth, the big veins in the neck flatten when you stand. In space they stay full, because blood isn't pulled down. Full neck veins are normal up there, which is one more reason readings are compared with in-flight baselines.

## Does salt in food affect fluid shift?

Salt makes the body hold on to water. Too much salt in space food is linked to fluid and bone problems, so NASA has cut the salt in station food. Before landing, though, extra salt with water is used deliberately to rebuild blood volume.
