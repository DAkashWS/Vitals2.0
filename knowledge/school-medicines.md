---
title: School medicines and first aid
source: VITALS 2.0 school health guide (general guidance; always follow local first-aid training and each student's care plan)
---

## Can a school give my child medicine?
Usually yes, if a parent or carer has given written consent. The form should say the medicine, the dose, when to give it and how. Staff check the label and record every dose. Schools won't give medicine without that permission, except in an emergency.

## How should medicines be brought into school?
In the original pharmacy container, with the pharmacy label showing the student's name, the dose and instructions, and an expiry date. Don't send loose tablets in envelopes or unlabelled bottles.

## Where are medicines kept at school?
Most are stored safely and out of reach, some in a fridge. Emergency medicines such as asthma reliever inhalers and adrenaline auto-injectors must never be locked away: they stay with the student or in a place everyone knows, and go with them to PE and on trips.

## Can students share medicine?
No. A medicine that is safe for one student can harm another, because of allergies, other conditions or the wrong dose. Students should never give or take someone else's medicine.

## Can children take aspirin?
Aspirin should not be given to anyone under 16 unless a doctor prescribes it, because it is linked to Reye's syndrome, a rare but serious illness that affects the liver and brain. Paracetamol or ibuprofen are used instead, only with parent consent and the right dose for age.

## What is an individual health care plan?
A written plan for a student with a long-term condition such as asthma, severe allergies, diabetes or epilepsy. It's made with the student, parents and doctor, and says what the condition is, daily needs, what an emergency looks like and exactly what to do. Staff who teach the student should know it.

## What should I do in an asthma attack?
Signs: coughing, wheezing, tight chest, breathlessness, struggling to speak. Help the student sit up straight and stay calm. Give their blue reliever inhaler, ideally with a spacer, as their plan says (often 1 puff every 30 to 60 seconds, up to 10 puffs). If they don't improve, are too breathless to talk, or you're worried, call an ambulance and keep giving the reliever.

## What is anaphylaxis and how do I use an EpiPen?
Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening allergic reaction, often to nuts, other foods, insect stings or medicines. Signs: swelling of the lips, face, tongue or throat, difficulty breathing, wheezing, hoarse voice, feeling faint, collapse. Give the adrenaline auto-injector (EpiPen) into the outer thigh straight away, call an ambulance and say "anaphylaxis". Lie them flat with legs raised (or sitting if breathing is hard). Give a second dose after 5 minutes if there's no improvement.

## What do I do if a diabetic student has a hypo?
A hypo is low blood sugar: shaky, sweaty, pale, hungry, confused, grumpy or drowsy. Give fast-acting sugar such as glucose tablets, a sugary (not diet) drink or juice. Wait 10 to 15 minutes and recheck; repeat if still low, then give a starchy snack. Never leave them alone or send them anywhere by themselves. If they are unconscious, put them in the recovery position and call an ambulance; give nothing by mouth.

## What should I do during a seizure?
Stay calm and time it. Move hard or sharp objects away and cushion their head. Don't hold them down or put anything in their mouth. When it stops, put them in the recovery position and stay with them until they're fully awake. Call an ambulance if it lasts more than 5 minutes, if seizures follow each other, if they're injured, if they have trouble breathing, or if it's their first seizure.

## When should a sick child stay home from school?
Students with a high temperature, who are clearly unwell, or who are vomiting or have diarrhoea should stay home. A common rule is to stay off until 48 hours after the last episode of vomiting or diarrhoea, so the bug doesn't spread around the class.

## What should I do for a nosebleed?
Sit the student down and lean them forward (not back). Pinch the soft part of the nose just above the nostrils for 10 to 15 minutes and have them breathe through the mouth. Get medical help if it lasts more than 20 minutes, is very heavy, or followed a hard knock to the head.

## How do I treat a burn?
Cool the burn under cool or lukewarm running water for 20 minutes. Remove jewellery or clothing near it unless stuck to the skin. Cover loosely with cling film or a clean plastic bag. Don't use ice, butter, toothpaste or creams. Large, deep, or face, hand or genital burns need hospital care.

## How do I treat a cut or graze?
Put on gloves. Press on the wound with a clean pad to stop bleeding. Rinse with clean water, pat dry and cover with a plaster or dressing. Deep, gaping or dirty wounds, bleeding that won't stop after 10 minutes of pressure, or bites need medical help.

## What do I do if someone is choking?
If they can cough, encourage them to keep coughing. If they can't cough, speak or breathe: give up to 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades, then up to 5 abdominal thrusts (not for babies). Repeat, and call an ambulance if it doesn't clear. If they become unresponsive, start CPR.

## How do I do CPR?
If someone collapses and isn't breathing normally, call emergency services and send someone for the defibrillator (AED). Push hard and fast in the centre of the chest, about 100 to 120 compressions a minute, letting the chest come back up each time. If trained, give 2 rescue breaths after every 30 compressions. Turn on the AED as soon as it arrives and follow its spoken instructions.

## What should be in a school first aid kit?
Disposable gloves, sterile dressings and plasters of different sizes, bandages, triangular bandages, safety pins, sterile eye pads, cleansing wipes, a foil blanket, scissors and a guidance leaflet. Check it regularly and restock after use. Medicines are not usually kept in a first-aid kit.

## Can a student take period pain medicine at school?
Yes, if parents have given written consent and the school's policy allows it. A warm pack, gentle movement, water and rest can also help. Severe pain that makes a student miss school regularly should be checked by a doctor.
