## How much radiation do astronauts get on the ISS?

Astronauts are exposed to about 72 millisieverts (mSv) on a six-month mission to the International Space Station.

## How much radiation would a Mars mission give?

A longer, three-year mission to Mars could expose astronauts to more than 1000 mSv. Without the protection of Earth's magnetic field, the rate of exposure rises dramatically.

## At what dose does radiation raise cancer risk?

The risk of cancer from ionizing radiation is well documented at doses of 100 mSv and above. A six-month ISS mission (about 72 mSv) is below that, and a three-year Mars mission (over 1000 mSv) is far above it.

## What is space radiation made of?

In space, astronauts are exposed mostly to high-energy protons, helium nuclei (alpha particles) and heavy, high-atomic-number ions called HZE ions. They also receive secondary radiation, made when those particles collide with the spacecraft or with body tissue.

## What are HZE ions?

HZE ions are heavy, highly charged, high-energy atomic nuclei in space radiation. The prominent ones are carbon, oxygen, magnesium, silicon and iron. Galactic cosmic rays are mostly very energetic protons with a small share of HZE ions, and both are important contributors to the dose astronauts receive.

## How is space radiation different from X-rays?

X-rays and gamma rays on Earth are "low-LET" radiation (low linear energy transfer). Space radiation is "high-LET": the way it ionizes molecules, cells and tissues is different, so its biological effects are different too.

## How do we know radiation causes cancer?

From studies of people exposed to radiation: survivors of the atomic bombs in Hiroshima and Nagasaki, nuclear reactor workers, radiotherapy patients, and people exposed by accident, such as at Chernobyl. They received low-LET doses in the same 50 to 2000 mSv range. These studies show strong evidence of cancer illness and death at more than 12 tissue sites.

## Which cancers does radiation cause most?

In the adults studied, the largest risks are several types of leukemia (including myeloid leukemia) and lymphoma, and tumours of the lung, breast, stomach, colon, bladder and liver.

## Is radiation cancer risk different for men and women?

Yes. Differences between the sexes are very likely due to the different natural rates of cancer in males and females. Females also carry extra risk for cancers of the breast, ovaries and lungs.

## Does age change radiation cancer risk?

There is evidence that the risk of radiation-caused cancer falls with increasing age, but how much it falls above the age of 30 is uncertain.

## Does space radiation cause the same cancers as X-rays?

It's unknown. High-LET space radiation may not cause the same types of tumours as low-LET radiation, and differences should be expected.

## What is RBE (relative biological effectiveness)?

RBE compares radiation types: it's the ratio of a dose of high-LET radiation to the dose of X-rays or gamma rays that produces the same biological effect. Studies of mice exposed to neutrons found that RBE varies with the type of tissue and the strain of mouse, which is why the tumours from space radiation are expected to differ.
