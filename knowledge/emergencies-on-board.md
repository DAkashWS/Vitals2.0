source: NASA (International Space Station emergency procedures)

## What are the main emergencies on a space station?

NASA plans for three main emergencies on the International Space Station: a fire, a rapid loss of cabin pressure (depressurisation), and a toxic atmosphere, such as an ammonia leak from the cooling system. The crew trains for all three and follows the station's emergency procedures, gathering at an agreed location and, if needed, preparing to leave in their docked spacecraft.

## How does fire behave in space?

In weightlessness, hot air doesn't rise, so flames form a rounded, often blue ball instead of a tall flame, and smoke spreads in every direction rather than going up. Fire can be harder to see and can spread along surfaces in unexpected ways. Smoke detectors watch the air, and the crew has fire extinguishers and breathing masks to use at once if the alarm sounds.

## What should I do if the fire alarm goes off?

Follow the station's fire procedure: put on a breathing mask from the emergency kit, gather with the crew at the agreed safe location, and help the commander find and fight the fire as trained. Don't breathe the smoke. After any fire, everyone should be checked for smoke inhalation, and anyone coughing, short of breath or confused needs the flight surgeon.

## What happens if the cabin loses pressure?

A leak lets air escape into space. A small leak is found and sealed while the crew watches the pressure. In a rapid loss of pressure, the crew follows the depressurisation procedure: gather, close hatches to isolate the leaking module, and be ready to leave in the docked spacecraft. Early signs of low pressure or low oxygen are ear popping, headache, dizziness and confusion.

## What is an ammonia leak and why is it dangerous?

The ISS uses ammonia to carry heat away from the station through its outside radiators. If it leaked into the cabin, it would irritate the eyes, nose, throat and lungs and could be life-threatening. The crew would put on breathing masks, move to a safe module and close the hatches. A strong sharp smell, stinging eyes or a burning feeling when breathing are warning signs.

## How do you do CPR in weightlessness?

Chest compressions are hard in weightlessness because pushing on someone just pushes you both apart. On the ISS, the patient is strapped to a medical restraint and the rescuer anchors themselves. Without a restraint, one method is to wrap your legs around the patient's chest and back and squeeze with your arms (the Evetts-Russomano method). Another is to put your feet against a wall, your hands on the patient's chest, and the patient's back against the opposite wall (the handstand method). Follow your training and the flight surgeon.

## What emergency equipment is on board?

Spacecraft carry fire extinguishers, breathing masks with their own oxygen, emergency medical kits, a defibrillator, and supplies for each type of emergency. Every crew member should know where the nearest mask, extinguisher and medical kit are in each module.
