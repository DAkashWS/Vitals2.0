## What is a spacewalk?

A spacewalk, or EVA (extravehicular activity), is any time an astronaut leaves the spacecraft in a spacesuit to work outside, for example to repair or install equipment. ISS spacewalks often last six to eight hours. They are some of the most physically demanding and risky tasks of a mission.

## Why do astronauts breathe pure oxygen before a spacewalk?

Spacesuits run at a much lower pressure than the cabin (the US suit at about 4.3 psi, less than a third of sea-level pressure), so they're easier to move in. Going from high to low pressure quickly can cause decompression sickness, "the bends", as nitrogen dissolved in the body forms bubbles. Breathing pure oxygen beforehand (the "prebreathe") washes nitrogen out of the body.

## What are the signs of decompression sickness (the bends)?

Joint pain (often in the shoulders, elbows or knees), tingling or numbness, an itchy or blotchy rash, unusual tiredness, headache, dizziness, confusion or trouble breathing during or after a spacewalk can all be signs of decompression sickness. Report any of them straight away: it's treated by raising the pressure again, and the flight surgeon must be told.

## Why do fingers and nails hurt after a spacewalk?

Pressurised spacesuit gloves are stiff, and gripping tools for hours against that stiffness tires the hands. Fingertips pressing on the glove ends can bruise the nail beds, and some astronauts have even lost fingernails after spacewalks. Trimming nails short beforehand and well-fitted gloves help. Numbness or lasting pain should be reported.

## What does space smell like?

Astronauts coming back in from a spacewalk often describe a smell on their suits and tools like seared steak, hot metal or welding fumes. It's thought to come from reactive oxygen atoms clinging to the surfaces. It's harmless and fades quickly.

## How do astronauts stay safe outside the spacecraft?

Spacewalkers are always attached by safety tethers, move hand over hand along handrails, and have a small jetpack (SAFER on US suits) to fly back if they drift free. Every spacewalk is planned in detail, and a crewmate inside and mission control monitor suit pressure, oxygen, CO2, temperature and heart rate the whole time.

## Can you get hot or cold in a spacesuit?

Yes. In sunlight, surfaces outside can get hotter than boiling water, and in Earth's shadow they can drop far below freezing, changing every 45 minutes or so in orbit. The suit's cooling garment, with water flowing through thin tubes, keeps the body comfortable, but hands and feet can still get cold. Tell the team if you're too hot, too cold, or your helmet fogs.
