---
title: Cabin air, water and food
source: NASA (International Space Station life support)
---

## Why does CO2 matter on a spacecraft?

Every breath adds carbon dioxide (CO2) to the cabin air, and in a closed spacecraft it has to be removed by life-support scrubbers. CO2 levels on spacecraft are usually higher than on Earth. Raised CO2 is linked to headaches, tiredness and trouble concentrating, so check the CO2 reading when crew members report headaches.

## How high is CO2 on the ISS?

CO2 on the ISS is usually several times higher than outdoor air on Earth (about 420 ppm), because scrubbers can only remove it so fast. NASA's crew limit is around 3 mmHg, roughly 4,000 ppm. Astronauts often report headaches, tiredness and trouble concentrating when levels rise, even within the limits, so it's worth checking the CO2 reading whenever several crew members feel unwell at once.

## Why do I get headaches in space?

Headaches are one of the most common complaints in space. The main causes are raised CO2 in the cabin, the fluid shift that puts pressure on the head in the first days, poor sleep, dehydration and eye strain. Check the CO2 reading first, drink water, rest and improve airflow around you. A sudden severe headache, or one with vision changes or confusion, needs the flight surgeon now.

## What CO2 levels are safe in VITALS?

VITALS uses draft ranges for cabin CO2: green below 1000 ppm, yellow from 1000 to 2000 ppm, and red above 2000 ppm. These are draft values for the demo and are being checked against NASA sources.

## Where does drinking water come from in space?

On the International Space Station, the water recovery system recycles water from cabin humidity (including sweat and breath) and from urine, cleaning it into drinking water. NASA reported in 2023 that the station's system recovers about 98 percent of the water the crew uses.

## How much water should I drink in space?

VITALS treats 2.5 to 3.5 litres a day as green and 1.8 to 4.0 litres as the yellow watch range. Because the fluid shift reduces thirst, drink on a schedule rather than waiting until you feel thirsty, and check urine colour.

## What do astronauts eat?

Astronauts eat mostly prepared foods: dehydrated meals that are rehydrated with water, thermostabilised (heat-treated) foods in pouches, and some fresh food when supply ships arrive. Tortillas are used instead of bread because they don't make crumbs that float into equipment. Good nutrition, enough calories, vitamin D and calcium help protect bones and health.

## Why do astronauts take vitamin D?

On Earth, the skin makes vitamin D in sunlight. Inside a spacecraft there's no sunlight on the skin, so astronauts take vitamin D supplements to protect their bones and immune system.

## Why is space food low in salt?

NASA has reduced the salt in ISS food. A lot of salt in the diet is linked to more calcium loss from bones, and it may also play a part in fluid and eye changes in space. Lower salt is better for bones, blood pressure and the kidneys.

## Can crumbs and drinks be dangerous in space?

Crumbs and droplets float and can get into eyes, lungs and equipment, so space food avoids crumbs (tortillas instead of bread) and drinks come in pouches with straws. Salt and pepper are liquids: salt dissolved in water and pepper suspended in oil.

## Do astronauts drink their own urine?

Yes, after it's cleaned. On the ISS, urine and condensed sweat and breath go through a recycling system that turns them into drinking water that's cleaner than many tap waters on Earth. It's tested regularly. Recycling water means much less has to be launched from Earth.
