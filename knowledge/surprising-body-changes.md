---
title: Surprising things space does to your body
source: NASA Human Research Program; Wikipedia, "Effect of spaceflight on the human body"
---

## Why am I taller in space?

Without gravity squeezing the discs between your vertebrae, your spine stretches out. Astronauts can grow up to about 3 percent taller in space, a few centimetres for most people. It's harmless, but it's one cause of back pain in the first weeks, and suits or seats fitted on Earth can feel tight. You shrink back after landing.

## Why is the skin on my feet peeling?

In weightlessness you don't walk, so the thick skin on the soles of your feet softens and peels away, leaving them soft like a baby's. Meanwhile the tops of your feet can get rough and calloused from hooking them under foot restraints and handrails. Moisturise, and clean up loose skin flakes so they don't float around the cabin.

## What happens if I cry in space?

Tears don't fall in weightlessness. They collect into a ball of liquid that stays stuck to your eye and can sting. Wipe them away with a cloth or tissue. The same surface tension makes sweat pool on your skin instead of dripping, especially during exercise.

## What is space anemia?

A 2022 study of 14 astronauts found their bodies destroyed about 54 percent more red blood cells in space than on Earth: around 3 million per second instead of 2 million. This "space anemia" can leave you tired and short of energy on long missions, and it can continue for a while after landing. It's one reason a good diet, rest and regular health checks matter.

## Does my heart change in space?

Yes. Without gravity, the heart doesn't have to pump blood "uphill" to the head, so it works a little less hard. Studies have found it becomes slightly more rounded in shape during long flights, and some heart muscle can be lost without exercise. It returns to normal shape after landing. Regular cardio exercise helps keep it strong.

## Is my immune system weaker in space?

Spaceflight affects the immune system. Stress, radiation, poor sleep and weightlessness all play a part. NASA has found that dormant viruses, like those that cause cold sores, chickenpox and shingles, reactivate in more than half of astronauts, though most of the time without symptoms. Report cold sores, a new rash or unusual tiredness to the flight surgeon.

## Why does my body temperature run higher in space?

A study of astronauts on the ISS found that their core body temperature at rest rose by about 1 °C over the first couple of months, a "space fever" with no illness. In weightlessness, sweat evaporates poorly and heat leaves the body less easily, and during exercise core temperature can climb above 40 °C. Keep cool during exercise, drink water, and compare temperature readings with your own in-flight baseline.

## Why does food taste bland in space?

The fluid shift toward the head gives you a blocked nose, much like a cold, and smell is a big part of taste. Many astronauts find food tastes dull and start loving spicy food, hot sauce and strong flavours. It usually improves somewhat as the body adapts.

## Is there a higher risk of kidney stones in space?

Yes. As bones lose calcium in weightlessness, more calcium passes through the kidneys, and less drinking makes urine more concentrated. Both raise the risk of kidney stones. Drinking enough water and exercising help prevent them. Severe pain in your side or back that comes in waves, or blood in your urine, needs the flight surgeon.

## Why is my voice different in space?

The fluid shift can make your face, nose and throat feel congested, so some astronauts sound slightly more nasal, as if they have a cold. It's usually harmless and settles as you adapt.

## Is it loud on a spacecraft? Can the noise affect my hearing?

Spacecraft are noisy: fans, pumps and life-support equipment run all the time. Long exposure to that noise can affect hearing, so crews wear hearing protection when it's loud, especially while sleeping and exercising near equipment, and have their hearing checked during the mission.
