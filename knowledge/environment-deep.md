title: The station environment, in depth
source: NASA, ESA, Roscosmos and published studies, gathered by team Mission 61:4
---

## How is oxygen made on the space station?

Mostly by splitting water. The Oxygen Generation System and the Russian Elektron pass electricity through water, which breaks it into oxygen for the cabin and hydrogen. Pressurised oxygen tanks brought by cargo ships are the backup.

## What is the Sabatier reactor?

Making oxygen from water leaves hydrogen behind. On the US side, a Sabatier reactor combines it with the CO2 the crew breathes out. The reaction makes water, which goes back into the water system, and methane, which is vented into space. It's a small, real example of the recycling a Mars ship will need.

## How is CO2 removed from the cabin air?

Fans pull cabin air through beds of zeolite, a mineral full of tiny pores that traps CO2. The beds are then heated and opened to space to release it, and used again. The Russian segment's system is called Vozdukh, and lithium hydroxide canisters are a backup that soaks CO2 up chemically.

## What are oxygen candles?

Solid-fuel oxygen generators, nicknamed candles, release oxygen from a chemical block when lit. They're an emergency backup. In 1997 one caught fire on the Mir station, filling it with smoke: a reminder that even backup systems need watching.

## Is the air pressure on the ISS the same as on Earth?

Yes, about 101 kPa, the same as sea level, with roughly the same mix of nitrogen and oxygen. Early US spacecraft used pure oxygen at low pressure, but after the Apollo 1 fire in 1967, crews moved to an Earth-like mix on the ground and before launch.

## What does space smell like?

Astronauts who come in from a spacewalk say their suits and tools smell like seared steak, hot metal or welding fumes. Inside, the station smells of people, food, machines and cleaning wipes. Fresh fruit arriving on a cargo ship is a big event, partly because of the smell.

## Why does the station sound so loud?

Fans, pumps and air conditioning run all day and night, because without them CO2 would pool and nothing would cool. Noise has been a long-running problem, especially in older modules. Crews use earplugs or noise-cancelling headphones to sleep, and their hearing is tested during the mission.

## Do germs and fungus grow on the space station?

Yes. Bacteria and fungi from the crew live on handrails, filters and walls. On the old Mir station, fungus damaged rubber seals and even etched windows. ISS crews spend time each week cleaning surfaces with wipes and vacuuming filters, and samples are sent back to Earth to check.

## Where does dust go in weightlessness?

It doesn't settle, it floats. Skin flakes, hair, crumbs and clothing lint drift until the air flow carries them to filters. That's why filters are vacuumed regularly, and why it's easy to get something in your eye up there.

## What is off-gassing on a spacecraft?

New materials, glue, plastics and even people slowly release chemical vapours into the air. On Earth they blow away; in a sealed cabin they build up. A trace contaminant control system with activated charcoal and catalysts removes them, and every item flown is tested for what it gives off.

## How much of the water on the ISS is recycled?

About 98 % in recent years. Sweat and breath condensed out of the air, and urine, are cleaned into drinking water. Astronauts like to say yesterday's coffee becomes tomorrow's coffee. The water is tested in flight and is often cleaner than tap water.

## How is the cabin kept from getting too humid?

A condensing heat exchanger chills part of the air so water vapour condenses, like a cold glass on a hot day. The water goes to the recycling system. Too humid and mould can grow; too dry and skin, eyes and noses suffer.

## Why do the lights on the space station change colour?

The station's LED lights can shift their colour through the day: bluer and brighter in the morning to help you wake up, warmer and dimmer in the evening to help you sleep. With 16 sunrises a day outside, the lights are a big part of keeping the body clock steady.

## How hot and cold is it outside the station?

In direct sunlight, outside surfaces can reach over 120 °C, and in Earth's shadow they can fall below −150 °C. That swing happens every 90 minutes. Insulation and radiators that pump heat away with ammonia keep the inside comfortable.

## What is the South Atlantic Anomaly?

A region over South America and the South Atlantic where Earth's radiation belts dip closest to the surface. The station passes through it several times a day, and radiation detectors show a spike each time. It's also where cameras and computers are more likely to glitch.

## How much radiation does an ISS astronaut get?

Roughly 0.3 to 0.8 mSv a day, depending on solar activity and the station's position. That's around the same as several months of natural background radiation on Earth, every few days. Every crew member carries a personal dosimeter.

## Why do astronauts see flashes of light with their eyes closed?

Fast cosmic ray particles pass through the eye and trigger the retina or the visual parts of the brain. Astronauts see streaks, dots or stars, more often when trying to sleep. Apollo astronauts reported them first; they're harmless to notice, but they're a reminder of the radiation around you.

## What is space debris and is it dangerous?

Old satellites, rocket parts and fragments circle Earth at around 7–8 km per second, so even a paint flake hits like a bullet. The station has shields on its outer walls, and it fires its engines to dodge debris when a close pass is predicted. If something is too close to dodge in time, the crew shelters in their spacecraft, ready to leave.

## What happens if the station springs a leak?

Pressure sensors notice the drop. The crew gathers, closes hatches module by module to find the leaking one, then seals it. In 2018 a small hole in a Soyuz was found and sealed with epoxy and gauze, and a slow leak in a Russian tunnel has been monitored for years.

## What are the three big emergencies on the ISS?

Fire, rapid loss of pressure, and a toxic atmosphere (such as an ammonia leak). Every crew trains for all three, and every module has the equipment for them. For each one, the crew knows where to meet and what to put on.

## Why is ammonia dangerous on the space station?

Ammonia carries heat to the radiators outside. If it leaked inside, it would burn the eyes and lungs. The crew would put on masks, move to a safe module and close the hatches, then wait for the air to be checked.

## Why do astronauts sleep next to a fan?

In weightlessness, warm breath doesn't rise away. A bubble of your own exhaled CO2 can collect around your face as you sleep, which can cause headaches and a stuffy, sluggish feeling. Sleeping with a fan blowing nearby clears it away.

## Why is the Moon's dust dangerous?

Lunar dust has never been smoothed by wind or water, so its grains are sharp, jagged and clingy. On Apollo it scratched visors, jammed seals and irritated eyes and noses. Keeping dust out of the cabin is one of the big challenges for Moon bases.

## What is the air like on Mars?

Mostly CO2, at less than 1 % of Earth's air pressure, so you can't breathe it and water would boil away at body temperature. The soil also contains perchlorates, salts that are harmful to people. Everything brought inside from a Mars walk has to be kept clean.

## Do plants help clean the air on the station?

Not much yet: there are too few. But astronauts have grown lettuce, mustard greens, zinnias and chilli peppers, and caring for plants clearly lifts mood. Future long missions may use plants as part of the life support system.

## Can you hear sound in space?

Inside the station, yes, because there is air. Outside, there's no air to carry sound, so a spacewalker hears only what travels through their suit: their own breathing, the fans in the suit, and the radio. Tools touching the hull can be felt and faintly heard through the gloves.
