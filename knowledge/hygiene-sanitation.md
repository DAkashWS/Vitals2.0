---
title: Hygiene and sanitation in space
source: NASA (living and working on the ISS); Wikipedia, "Space toilet" and "ISS ECLSS"
---

## Why does hygiene matter more in space?

A spacecraft is a small, sealed home where the same air and water are recycled again and again, and nobody can step outside. Germs from one crew member reach everyone quickly, and spaceflight weakens the immune system. Good hygiene and regular cleaning are some of the best protections the crew has, because medical help is far away.

## How do you wash your hands in space?

There's no sink with running water. Astronauts clean their hands with wet wipes, no-rinse soap or hand sanitiser, rubbing every part of the hands for about 20 seconds, then drying them with a towel so no droplets float away. Clean your hands before eating or handling food, after using the toilet, after exercise, after cleaning, and before touching your eyes, nose or mouth.

## How often should I clean my hands?

Clean them at every key moment: before eating or preparing food, after the toilet, after exercise or touching shared equipment, after cleaning or handling waste, and before touching your face. That usually adds up to at least five times a day. VITALS treats 5 or more times a day as green and 3 to 4 as yellow (draft values for the demo).

## Are there germs and mould on the space station?

Yes. Studies of ISS surfaces found bacteria and fungi similar to those in homes and offices on Earth, mostly carried by the crew's own skin. NASA monitors the station's microbes, and the crew samples surfaces and air. The Russian Mir station had serious mould growing behind its panels, so crews watch for damp spots, condensation and anything that smells musty.

## How do astronauts clean the space station?

ISS crews do routine housekeeping, often on Saturdays: wiping handrails, tables, the toilet area and exercise equipment with disinfectant wipes, vacuuming air filters, and bagging rubbish. Shared equipment like exercise machines should be wiped down after each use, because sweat doesn't drip away in weightlessness.

## Why is humidity watched so closely?

Breath and sweat add water to the cabin air. The life-support system removes it and recycles it into drinking water, keeping humidity at a comfortable level. Too damp, and condensation can form behind panels and grow mould. Too dry, and skin, eyes and nose dry out and crack. Report any condensation, damp patches or musty smells.

## How do you use the toilet hygienically in space?

Space toilets use airflow instead of gravity: a fan pulls urine into a funnel and hose, and solid waste into a container with a liner. On the ISS, urine is recycled into drinking water, and solid waste is sealed and stored. Clean the seat and funnel area after use, close lids and valves properly, and clean your hands afterwards.

## Where does the rubbish go on the space station?

Rubbish, food packaging and solid waste are sealed in bags and stowed. They're loaded into cargo spacecraft that have finished delivering supplies, which then burn up as they re-enter Earth's atmosphere. Until then, bag waste tightly so nothing floats out, and keep wet waste sealed to stop smells and germs.

## How is drinking water kept clean in space?

On the ISS, water recovered from urine and cabin humidity is filtered, processed and treated before anyone drinks it, and it's tested regularly. The US system adds iodine and the Russian system adds silver to stop bacteria growing. If water looks cloudy, smells or tastes strange, don't drink it: report it to the crew and use stored water.

## How is food kept safe in space?

Space food is sterilised, heat-treated or freeze-dried so it keeps without a fridge. Clean your hands and the food area before eating, use clean utensils, and catch crumbs and droplets. Bag leftovers and packaging straight away, and wipe the table afterwards. Don't eat anything that's swollen, damaged, past its date or smells wrong.

## Why do astronauts quarantine before launch?

Crews spend about two weeks in health stabilisation before launch, meeting as few people as possible, so they don't carry a cold, flu or other infection into space. An infection that starts in a sealed spacecraft can spread to the whole crew.

## What should I do if a crewmate is ill?

Keep apart where you can, don't share towels, cups or utensils, and clean shared surfaces more often. The ill crew member should wear a mask if they're coughing or sneezing, and clean their hands often. Tell the flight surgeon, especially if they have a fever, vomiting or diarrhoea.

## Why should I look after my teeth and skin in space?

There's no dentist or dermatologist on board, and small problems can become big ones on a long mission. Brush twice a day and floss daily, moisturise dry skin, keep cuts clean and covered, and report sore gums, a toothache, a rash or a wound that looks infected early.
