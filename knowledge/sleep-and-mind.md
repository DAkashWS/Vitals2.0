---
title: Sleep, mood and isolation
source: NASA Human Research Program
---

## Why is it hard to sleep in space?

The International Space Station orbits Earth about every 90 minutes, so the crew sees around 16 sunrises and sunsets a day. That confuses the body clock. Noise, light, a busy schedule, excitement and a different sleeping position add to it. Many astronauts sleep less in space than on Earth.

## How do astronauts sleep in space?

Astronauts sleep in small crew quarters inside a sleeping bag attached to the wall, so they don't drift. They use eye masks and earplugs, and the station uses special lighting that changes colour and brightness to help keep the body clock on schedule.

## How much sleep should I get?

VITALS treats 7 to 9 hours a night as green and 6 to 10 hours as the yellow watch range. Regular sleep and wake times, dim light before bed and no caffeine in the 6 hours before sleep help.

## How does isolation affect astronauts?

Living for months in a small space with the same few people, far from home, can leave astronauts feeling lonely, homesick or bored, and can lead to low mood, irritability, poor sleep, tiredness and friction within the crew. On long missions with delayed messages, it can be harder still.

## How much do astronauts actually sleep in space?

A large study of shuttle and ISS crews found astronauts averaged only about six hours of sleep a night in space, even though more was scheduled, and about three quarters of ISS crew members used sleep medicine at some point. So poor sleep in space is very common, and it isn't a personal failing. Sleep medicine should only be taken as the flight surgeon directs.

## Can CO2 build up around my head while I sleep?

Yes. In weightlessness, warm breath doesn't rise and drift away. Without airflow, the CO2 you breathe out can collect in a pocket around your face, which can cause headaches and poor sleep. Make sure the ventilation fan in your crew quarters is on and nothing blocks it, and sleep with your face near the airflow.

## Is it okay to nap in space?

Short naps of about 20 to 30 minutes can help if you slept badly, as long as they don't push back your normal bedtime. Avoid long naps late in the day. Keep a regular sleep and wake time as much as the schedule allows.

## Why do I feel low in the middle of a long mission?

Studies of crews in polar stations and some space missions describe a "third-quarter phenomenon": morale and mood often dip after the halfway point, when the novelty has worn off and the end still feels far away. Knowing it's common helps. Plan things to look forward to, keep routines, and talk to crewmates or the mission psychologist.

## What is the overview effect?

Many astronauts describe a powerful shift in feeling when they see the whole Earth from space: a sense of how small and fragile the planet is, and of everyone on it being connected. It's called the overview effect, and many astronauts say it changed how they see life back home.

## What can I do when I'm bored in space?

Long missions have quiet stretches. Astronauts read, watch films, play music, take photos of Earth, write, learn languages, play games with crewmates and call home when they can. Having a hobby and a weekly routine with something to look forward to helps keep mood up.

## How do I handle conflict with crewmates?

In a small crew far from home, small irritations can grow. Talk early and calmly, focus on the problem, not the person, and give each other private time. The commander and the mission psychologists can help. Crews are trained in teamwork and conflict resolution before flight.

## What helps with stress and loneliness in space?

A regular routine, enough sleep, exercise, private time, hobbies, talking openly with crewmates, and staying in touch with family and friends all help. Crews can also talk privately with mission psychologists. If low mood lasts or affects work or safety, tell the flight surgeon.
