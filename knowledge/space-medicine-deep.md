---
title: Medicine far from a hospital, in depth
source: NASA, ESA, Roscosmos and published studies, gathered by team Mission 61:4
---

## Who is the doctor on board the space station?

Usually nobody is a full doctor. Each crew has crew medical officers who get extra emergency medical training before flight. Flight surgeons on the ground guide them through anything serious over the radio and video link.

## What medical equipment is on the ISS?

A medical kit with first aid supplies, diagnostic tools, a defibrillator, an ultrasound machine, oxygen, and equipment for breathing support and eye exams. There's also a restraint system to hold a patient and a helper still while they work.

## How do you do CPR in weightlessness?

You can't just push down, because you'd float away. On the station the patient is strapped to a restraint board and the rescuer straps in too. Other methods tested include bracing your feet against the ceiling, or wrapping your legs around the patient's chest.

## Has anyone been evacuated from space for a medical reason?

Yes. In 1985 the Soviet Salyut 7 mission was cut short when cosmonaut Vladimir Vasyutin became seriously ill, and the crew returned early. From the ISS, the crew's spacecraft is always docked as a lifeboat for an emergency return.

## How long would an emergency return from the ISS take?

From the decision to leave, a crew can be back on Earth in a matter of hours, typically under a day. From the Moon it would take days, and from Mars it isn't possible at all, which is why deep-space crews need far more medical independence.

## Could surgery be done in space?

Only very simple procedures so far. In weightlessness, blood and fluids float into droplets instead of pooling, which makes surgery much harder. Small surgical robots have been tested on the ISS, and enclosed surgical boxes are being studied for the future.

## Do medicines expire faster in space?

Some may. A NASA study found that some medicines stored on the ISS lost potency earlier than on Earth, probably because of radiation and storage conditions. Medicine for a Mars mission will need to be tested and protected carefully.

## Do bacteria behave differently in space?

Some do. Experiments have found that certain bacteria, like Salmonella, became more able to cause disease after growing in space. That's why hygiene, cleaning and checking the station's microbes are taken seriously.

## What are the most common health complaints in space?

Headaches, back pain, congestion, skin rashes, sleep problems, small cuts and bruises, and something in the eye. Most are minor, but they're logged so the flight surgeon can spot patterns across the crew.

## Why are eye injuries common in space?

Dust, crumbs and metal shavings float instead of falling, and can drift into an eye. Astronauts wear eye protection for some jobs, and the medical kit has ways to rinse the eye with clean water.

## Do wounds heal more slowly in space?

Studies suggest healing may be slower in weightlessness, and the immune system works differently. Keeping cuts clean and covered, and reporting any sign of infection early, matters even more than on Earth.

## Why do astronauts go into quarantine before launch?

To stop them taking an illness into space. About two weeks before launch, crews limit their contact with other people. In 1970, Apollo 13 pilot Ken Mattingly was replaced days before launch after being exposed to measles.

## What happened on Apollo 7 with colds?

The Apollo 7 crew caught head colds in flight in 1968. In weightlessness, mucus doesn't drain from the sinuses, and the crew were famously uncomfortable and grumpy. It showed how even minor illness is harder in space.

## How do teeth get treated in space?

There's no dentist on board, so crews have dental checks before flight and get training in basic dental first aid. The medical kit has supplies for temporary fillings and pain relief, used as the flight surgeon directs.

## How is blood tested on the space station?

Astronauts draw blood from each other, then spin it in a centrifuge and freeze it. Many samples are sent back to Earth on cargo ships for analysis. Smaller devices that test blood on the spot are being developed.

## How would a Mars crew get medicine?

They'd have to carry everything, or make it. Researchers are studying how to grow medicine-producing microbes and plants and how to 3D-print tablets on demand, because a resupply ship could take many months.

## Why is telemedicine so important in space?

Flight surgeons can't be on board, so they see the crew through video, data and guided scans. On the ISS this works almost live. On Mars, with up to 44 minutes for a question and answer, crews will need more decision support on board, which is exactly what VITALS is for.

## Has anyone been pregnant in space?

No. Pregnancy isn't allowed on missions, and very little is known about how weightlessness and radiation would affect it. Research so far has only used animals and cells.

## How do medical teams keep crew health private?

Astronaut medical data is confidential. Private medical conferences with the flight surgeon happen on a private channel, away from the rest of mission control. Crews can talk openly, which is important for catching problems early.

## Why is hearing tested in space?

The station's constant fan and pump noise can damage hearing over months. Astronauts take regular hearing tests and wear hearing protection in noisy areas and for sleep.

## How are medical emergencies practised for space?

Crews rehearse emergencies on Earth and in orbit, using mannequins and simulations: CPR, burns, eye injuries, broken bones and allergic reactions. Practising in advance means no one has to work out what to do for the first time in a real emergency.
