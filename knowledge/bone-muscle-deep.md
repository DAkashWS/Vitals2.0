---
title: Bones and muscles, in depth
source: NASA, ESA and published studies, gathered by team Mission 61:4
---

## How fast can muscle waste away in space?

Very fast. NASA has reported astronauts losing up to 20 % of muscle mass on flights of just 5 to 11 days without enough exercise. The muscles that hold you upright on Earth, like the calves and back muscles, are hit hardest.

## Which muscles are affected most in space?

The postural muscles: calves (especially the soleus), thighs, glutes and the deep muscles along the spine. They usually work all day against gravity, so in weightlessness they have almost nothing to do and shrink first.

## How does the space treadmill work?

Astronauts wear a harness attached to bungee cords or a loading system that pulls them down onto the belt, so they can run without floating away. They usually run with a load that's only part of their body weight, so building it up over time matters.

## How do you ride an exercise bike without a seat?

The station's bike, CEVIS, has no real need for a seat: astronauts clip their shoes into the pedals and strap in at the waist, or just pedal while floating. It trains the heart and lungs, which also weaken in space.

## How heavy can the space weightlifting machine go?

The ISS's Advanced Resistive Exercise Device uses vacuum cylinders instead of weights, and can provide up to about 270 kg of resistance. Squats, deadlifts and heel raises on it help protect both bones and muscles.

## Why is back pain so common in space?

The spine lengthens as the discs expand without gravity, stretching the back muscles and ligaments. Many astronauts get lower back pain in the first days. Curling up into a gentle crouch often eases it.

## Are astronauts at risk of slipped discs after flight?

Yes. Studies have found that astronauts are several times more likely to get a herniated disc in the first year after landing than other people, as the spine readjusts to gravity. Careful lifting and core exercises matter after return.

## What is the neutral body posture?

When fully relaxed in weightlessness, the body curls into a crouch: knees and hips bent, arms floating up in front. Workstations and sleeping bags are designed around it, so astronauts don't fight their own posture all day.

## Do the soles of astronauts' feet change?

Yes. Without walking, the thick skin on the soles of the feet softens and peels away, sometimes floating off in flakes. Meanwhile the tops of the feet get rough patches from hooking under foot rails to stay in place.

## How long does bone take to recover after a mission?

Slowly, and often not completely. Studies of astronauts a year after landing found many had recovered only part of the bone they lost, particularly in the hips and legs. That's why exercise during the mission matters so much.

## Why are kidney stones a risk in space?

As bones lose calcium, more of it passes through the kidneys. Along with lower fluid intake, that raises the risk of kidney stones. Drinking enough water is one of the simplest ways to cut that risk.

## Why does vitamin D matter in space?

Without sunlight on the skin, the body makes little vitamin D, which bones need to use calcium. Astronauts' vitamin D levels are monitored, and diet is planned with this in mind.

## What were the "mighty mice" in space?

In 2019, mice sent to the ISS with a blocked muscle-limiting protein (myostatin) kept and even built muscle and bone while normal mice lost them. It's early research, but it may one day help astronauts and people on Earth with muscle-wasting diseases.

## Will the Moon's gravity be enough to protect bones?

Nobody knows yet. The Moon has a sixth of Earth's gravity and Mars about 38 %. Partial gravity may slow bone and muscle loss but probably won't stop it, so exercise will still be needed on both.

## How did Apollo astronauts move on the Moon?

Many found that hopping like a kangaroo, the "bunny hop", was easier than walking in low gravity and a stiff suit. Falling over was common but slow, and getting up again took some practice.

## What happens to balance after a long mission?

The inner ear and brain stop trusting gravity in space, so after landing, astronauts can feel unsteady and dizzy for days. Turning the head can make the room seem to swing. Balance training is part of rehabilitation.

## What is post-flight reconditioning?

After long missions, astronauts follow a supervised exercise programme of around 45 days, working with trainers on strength, balance, flexibility and endurance. Most return to their pre-flight fitness, but bone takes longer.

## Do tendons and ligaments change in space?

They can weaken too, along with the muscles they connect. Gradually building load back up after landing, rather than jumping straight into heavy exercise, lowers the risk of injury.

## How much exercise do astronauts do each day?

About two hours a day, six days a week, including setting up and cleaning equipment. It's a mix of running, cycling and resistance training, planned for each person.

## Why do astronauts lose grip strength and dexterity?

Hands do lots of fine work in space, but without gravity the forearm muscles still weaken somewhat. Spacewalks in stiff gloves are especially tiring for the hands. Grip exercises and good glove fit help.

## Does bone loss in space help people on Earth?

Yes. Spaceflight causes bone loss much faster than ageing does, so it's a useful way to study osteoporosis and test ways to slow it. Research on astronauts has helped understand bone loss in older people and in long hospital stays.
