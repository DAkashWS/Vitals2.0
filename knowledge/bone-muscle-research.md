## How does spaceflight affect the human body?

The effects are complex and mostly harmful, in the short and the long term. The biggest problems from long weightlessness are muscle wasting and bone loss (spaceflight osteopenia). Spaceflight also slows the cardiovascular system, reduces red blood cell production (space anemia), and causes balance problems, eyesight problems and immune changes.

## What other symptoms do astronauts get?

Fluids shift towards the head, giving the puffy "moon face" seen in photos of astronauts. Astronauts also lose body mass and can get a blocked nose, disturbed sleep and extra flatulence. A 2024 review listed bone loss, higher cancer risk, vision problems, weaker immunity and mental health issues as well-known problems, though what happens at a molecular level still isn't fully understood.

## Why is space research on private astronauts a concern?

Private and commercial spaceflight is now happening without scientific or medical research being done on those travellers, so their health effects aren't being studied. Researchers have raised this as a concern.

## How much muscle do astronauts lose, and why do muscles get weaker?

In weightlessness, astronauts put almost no weight on the back and leg muscles used for standing. Those muscles weaken and shrink. Without regular exercise, astronauts can lose up to 20% of their muscle mass in just 5 to 11 days.

## How do muscle fibres change in space?

The slow-twitch endurance fibres that hold your posture are replaced by fast-twitch fibres that contract quickly but aren't good for heavy work. Research on exercise, hormone supplements and medicines may help keep muscle and body mass.

## How much bone do astronauts lose in space?

Bone is normally built up along the lines of mechanical stress, and in microgravity there's very little. Astronauts lose about 1.5% of bone a month, especially from the lower spine, hips and thigh bone. On Earth, a healthy adult loses about 3% of cortical bone per decade; in microgravity it's about 1% every month.

## Why does bone break down faster in space?

On Earth, two kinds of cells keep bone balanced: osteoclasts break old bone down and osteoblasts build new bone, always together. In microgravity, osteoclasts become more active while osteoblasts don't keep up, so bone is lost without being replaced. This is strongest in the pelvis, which normally carries the most weight.

## What did the mouse study show about bone loss?

In healthy mice, after only 16 days in microgravity, osteoclasts (the bone-eating cells) increased by 197%, while osteoblasts and the growth factors that help form new bone went down.

## Can bone loss cause kidney stones?

Yes. Calcium released from lost bone raises blood calcium levels, which can lead to dangerous calcification of soft tissues and to kidney stones. Drinking enough water helps reduce the risk.

## Does bone grow back after spaceflight?

Unlike people with osteoporosis, astronauts eventually regain their bone density, but slowly: after a 3 to 4 month trip into space, it takes about 2 to 3 years. It's still not known whether bone fully recovers. Diet, exercise and medicines are being studied to speed it up.

## What exercise equipment is on the ISS (treadmills, ARED, bike)?

The ISS has two treadmills (including COLBERT), the ARED (Advanced Resistive Exercise Device) for weight-lifting exercises, and a stationary bike. Each astronaut exercises for at least two hours a day. On the treadmill, they strap themselves down with bungee cords so they don't float away.

## Does weight-lifting on the ARED protect bones?

According to this research, the ARED's weight-lifting exercises build muscle but don't stop bone density loss on their own, which is why astronauts combine different exercises and other countermeasures.

## Why do astronauts wear special trousers?

Astronauts on long missions wear trousers with elastic bands running from the waistband to the cuffs. They squeeze the leg bones to put some load on them and reduce bone loss (osteopenia).

## What is NASA's Digital Astronaut Project?

NASA's Human Research Program uses computer models to work out the best exercise to protect bone and muscle on long missions. The Digital Astronaut Project combines a model of the ARED exercise machine with OpenSim models of the human body, to estimate the forces on joints and muscles during each exercise. That helps prescribe better workouts and predict whether an exercise plan is enough to keep an astronaut's bones and muscles healthy.
