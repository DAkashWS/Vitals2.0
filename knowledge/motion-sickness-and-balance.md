---
title: Space motion sickness and balance
source: NASA Human Research Program
---

## What is space motion sickness?

In weightlessness, the balance organs in the inner ear no longer sense "down", so what they report doesn't match what the eyes see. This mismatch can cause nausea, vomiting, headache, sweating and tiredness. It's also called space adaptation syndrome. More than half of astronauts feel it in their first two to three days in space, and it usually eases as the brain adapts. Having it says nothing about how good an astronaut you are.

## Why is vomiting dangerous in a spacesuit?

Vomiting inside a helmet could block your airway and is extremely dangerous, which is why spacewalks aren't scheduled in the first days of a mission, when space motion sickness is most common. If you feel sick before or during a spacewalk, tell the team at once.

## Why do I feel upside down or confused about direction?

With no gravity, your brain decides what's "up" from what it sees: the floor, the ceiling, the lights. Floating into a module at an odd angle can suddenly flip your sense of direction, called a visual reorientation illusion. It can make you feel queasy. Orient yourself to the module's marked floor and lights, and move slowly.

## How can I reduce space motion sickness?

Move your head slowly and avoid quick turns, keep a consistent sense of "up" in the module, fix your eyes on a stable point, and sip water often. The flight surgeon can advise on motion sickness medicine from the kit.

## Why do astronauts feel wobbly after landing?

After weeks or months in weightlessness, the brain has to relearn how to use gravity for balance. Returning astronauts often feel dizzy or unsteady, find walking and turning hard, and can feel faint when standing because blood pools in the legs. Most of this improves within days to weeks.

## What changes when moving to the Moon or Mars?

Moving into partial gravity (about one sixth of Earth's on the Moon and about three eighths on Mars) means the body and balance system have to adapt again. Walking, jumping and landing feel different, and coordination can be off at first, which raises the risk of falls.
