## What is space medicine?

Space medicine is medicine practised under the conditions of spaceflight. It grew out of aviation medicine and is a subspecialty of emergency medicine. It's dedicated to preventing and treating medical conditions that could limit success in space, focusing on prevention, acute care, emergency medicine, wilderness medicine, and hyperbaric and hypobaric medicine.

## What stresses does spaceflight put on the body?

The spaceflight environment brings unique stressors: g-forces, microgravity, unusual atmospheres such as low pressure or high carbon dioxide, and space radiation. Space medicine studies their effects and uses preventive medicine, emergency care, fitness, nutrition, behavioural health and toxicology to shape spacecraft design and mission plans, and to prevent and treat medical problems in space.

## What is astronautical hygiene?

Astronautical hygiene is the use of science and technology to prevent or control exposure to hazards that could make astronauts ill. It works alongside space medicine to make sure astronauts work in a safe environment. Visual impairment and bone loss are among the medical effects linked to human spaceflight.

## Can astronauts get blood clots in space?

Yes. In 2020, a blood clot (deep vein thrombosis) was found in the jugular vein in the neck of an astronaut on a long ISS stay, and it was treated with blood thinners. A later study of eleven astronauts found slowed blood flow in the neck veins, and even blood flowing backwards in two of them. NASA is researching whether this makes clots more likely.

## Does spaceflight affect heart rhythm?

Heart rhythm disturbances have been seen in astronauts, mostly linked to heart disease, though it's unclear whether that came from existing conditions or from spaceflight. Better screening for heart disease has reduced the risk. Problems like atrial fibrillation can develop over time, so crew members' heart rhythms are checked regularly. There's some concern that long exposure to microgravity could disturb heart rhythm, though this hasn't been seen so far.

## Why do spacesuits use low pressure and pure oxygen?

Spacesuits are filled with 100% oxygen at less than a third of normal atmospheric pressure. Removing nitrogen lets the astronaut breathe comfortably, and the low pressure keeps the suit flexible enough to move the hands, arms and legs. A higher-pressure suit would be much stiffer to work in.

## What is pre-breathing before a spacewalk?

After putting on the suit, the air inside is replaced by 100% oxygen (the "nitrogen purge"). To avoid decompression sickness, the astronaut also spends several hours pre-breathing, so nitrogen leaves the body's tissues slowly enough that no bubbles form. After the spacewalk, the pressure is brought back to the spacecraft's normal level.

## What is decompression sickness (DCS)?

Decompression sickness is injury caused by nitrogen bubbles in the tissues and blood, when a fast drop in surrounding pressure makes dissolved nitrogen come out as gas. In space the risk is cut by breathing 100% oxygen before and after the nitrogen purge. It can be caused by too little or interrupted pre-breathing, dehydration, fitness, past injuries, age, a strenuous or long spacewalk, or a loss of suit or cabin pressure.

## What are the symptoms of decompression sickness in space?

Symptoms can include chest pain, shortness of breath, a cough or pain on deep breathing, unusual tiredness, light-headedness, dizziness, headache, unexplained muscle or joint pain, tingling or numbness, weak arms or legs, or problems with vision. Any of these during or after a spacewalk needs the flight surgeon straight away.

## How is decompression sickness (DCS) treated in space?

The main treatments are raising the pressure in the suit again so the nitrogen bubbles dissolve, breathing 100% oxygen to re-oxygenate the tissues, and drinking fluids to improve blood flow to injured tissues. The flight surgeon directs the treatment.

## What is barotrauma?

Barotrauma is injury to air-filled spaces in the body (the middle ears, sinuses, lungs and gut) caused by a pressure difference between those spaces and the air around them. A cold, nasal allergies, repeated pressure changes, dehydration or poor equalising technique make it more likely.

## What are the symptoms of barotrauma and how is it treated?

When pressure drops before a spacewalk, trapped air expands, causing a bloated stomach, ear or sinus pain, muffled hearing, or tooth and jaw pain. When pressure rises again afterwards, ear and sinus pain, muffled hearing and tooth or jaw pain are common. Treatments include releasing or equalising the pressure in the ears and sinuses, gentle massage for bloating, and decongestants, steroids or pain relief as the flight surgeon directs.

## Why is the immune system weaker in space?

In space, the immune system weakens: T-cells don't multiply properly and the ones that exist are less able to fight infection. Viruses already in the body, which are normally kept quiet, can wake up. NASA reported in 2019 that dormant viruses may be activated during missions, adding risk on deep-space trips.

## Are germs more dangerous in space?

They can be. A 2006 Space Shuttle experiment found that Salmonella, which causes food poisoning, became more virulent when grown in space. In 2013, NASA-funded researchers reported that microbes on the ISS adapt in ways not seen on Earth that can increase their growth and virulence, and in 2017 bacteria were found to be more resistant to antibiotics in near-weightlessness. Microbes on the ISS are monitored carefully to keep the crew healthy.

## How does fatigue affect astronauts?

Spaceflight often means long stretches without proper rest. Lack of sleep causes fatigue, which leads to mistakes on critical tasks, and tired people often can't tell how impaired they are. Sleep loss, shifting sleep times and overwork can cause errors that put the crew's health, safety and mission at risk.

## Why do astronauts lose their balance?

Leaving and returning to Earth's gravity causes space sickness, dizziness and loss of balance, involving the senses, the brain, the inner ear and blood pressure. For the trip home, NASA astronauts can use midodrine, an "anti-dizzy" medicine that temporarily raises blood pressure, and/or promethazine, only as the flight surgeon directs.

## Why do bones get weaker in space, and how much bone is lost?

Bone loss in spaceflight is called spaceflight osteopenia. Calcium leaks out of the bones in microgravity. After 3 to 4 months in space, it takes about 2 to 3 years to regain the lost bone density. Diet and exercise, vibration therapy, and medicines that boost bone growth are being studied to speed recovery.

## Why do muscles waste away in space?

Muscles in the legs, back, spine and heart weaken and shrink because they no longer have to work against gravity. Astronauts protect them with at least two hours a day of exercise, including resistance training, and researchers are testing electrical muscle stimulation to prevent muscle loss.

## What is SANS?

SANS (Spaceflight Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome) is a set of eye changes and vision problems that can develop on long space missions. It's a major concern for future deep-space missions, including a human mission to Mars.

## Does spaceflight affect the brain?

A NASA-supported study in 2012 reported that spaceflight may harm the brain and could speed up the onset of Alzheimer's disease. In 2017, MRI studies found significant changes in the position and structure of astronauts' brains, with bigger changes after longer trips.

## Do astronauts take sleeping pills?

Many do. A 10-year study found that 75% of ISS crew members and 78% of Space Shuttle crew members took sleep medicine in space, on about half of their nights. NASA schedules 8.5 hours for sleep on the ISS, but astronauts average only about 6 hours. Medicines are used only as the flight surgeon directs.

## How does light help astronauts sleep?

Light at different colours and brightness sets the body clock through receptors in the eye that are most sensitive to blue light (about 470 to 490 nm). The ISS has light panels that change through the day: brighter, bluer light in the "morning" for alertness, and red-tinted light (under 600 nm) at "night" so the crew can see without being kept awake.

## Does melatonin work in space?

Melatonin, a natural hormone made by the pineal gland, has helped astronauts fall asleep faster in orbit.

## What sleep medicines are used on the ISS (zolpidem, zopiclone, zaleplon, temazepam)?

The most commonly dispensed are the "z-drugs": zolpidem, zopiclone and zaleplon. They seem to leave less grogginess the next day than many older sleep medicines, and the shortest-acting one, zaleplon, causes little impairment even if taken an hour before waking. Benzodiazepines are also used, but longer-acting ones such as temazepam can leave crews groggy in the morning. All are used only as the flight surgeon directs.

## What is modafinil used for in space?

Modafinil is a wakefulness medicine kept on the space station to reduce the effects of disrupted sleep and help crews perform while tired. On Earth it has restored thinking to normal levels after a full night without sleep, but it hasn't been studied in astronauts. It's used only as the flight surgeon directs.

## How do doctors train in space medicine?

There are only three space medicine fellowships: at the University of Texas in Houston, UCLA and Harvard. Training covers acute care, flight medicine, emergency medicine, life support systems, hyperbaric and hypobaric medicine, public health, disaster medicine, and wilderness and extreme medicine. Space nursing is a related specialty that studies how space travel affects the body's responses.
