---
title: Fluid shift and vision
source: NASA Human Research Program
---

## Why does my face look puffy in space?

On Earth, gravity pulls body fluids toward the legs. In weightlessness, about two litres of fluid shift up toward the chest and head. That gives astronauts a puffy, slightly swollen face and thin legs, sometimes called "puffy face, bird legs". It usually settles somewhat as the body adapts over the first days and weeks. If your face, lips or throat swell suddenly, or it's hard to breathe, that can be an allergic reaction: get help now.

## Why is my nose stuffy and my head full in space?

The upward fluid shift can cause a blocked nose, a feeling of fullness or pressure in the head, and a dulled sense of taste and smell. Many astronauts find food tastes blander and prefer spicier food in space.

## Why do I feel less thirsty in space?

The body senses the extra fluid in the upper body and responds by reducing thirst and passing more urine. Astronauts can lose body water without noticing, so it helps to drink on a schedule and check urine colour.

## Can spaceflight affect my eyesight?

Yes. Some astronauts on long missions develop changes in the back of the eye and the optic nerve, called Spaceflight Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome (SANS). It can make near vision blurry. NASA checks crew eyes regularly in flight. Report any change in vision to the flight surgeon.
