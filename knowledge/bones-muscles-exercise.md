---
title: Bones, muscles and exercise
source: NASA Human Research Program
---

## Do bones get weaker in space?

Yes. Without the load of gravity, the body breaks down bone faster than it rebuilds it. Astronauts can lose about 1 to 1.5 percent of bone mass per month in weight-bearing bones like the hips and spine. Some of it may not fully recover after returning to Earth, and it raises the risk of fractures and kidney stones.

## Do muscles get weaker in space?

Yes. Muscles that hold us up against gravity, like those in the legs and back, weaken and shrink when they're not used. Without exercise, astronauts lose muscle strength and endurance within weeks.

## How much do astronauts exercise in space?

Astronauts on the International Space Station exercise about two hours a day, six days a week. They use a treadmill with a harness, an exercise bike and a resistance machine that works like lifting weights. Exercise protects bones, muscles, the heart and mood.

## What exercise machines are on the ISS?

The ISS has three main machines: a treadmill (T2), where bungee cords and a harness hold the runner down; an exercise bike without a seat or handlebars (CEVIS), which you clip into; and a resistance machine (ARED) that uses vacuum cylinders to simulate lifting weights like squats and deadlifts. The machines sit on vibration isolation systems so they don't shake the station.

## Why is exercise so important in space?

Exercise is the main protection against bone and muscle loss, keeps the heart and blood vessels fit, helps sleep and lifts mood. Heavy resistance exercise, like squats on the resistance machine, is the best protection for bones. Skipping exercise for a few days is fine if you're unwell, but tell the flight surgeon if you can't keep up the routine.

## What is a good exercise amount in VITALS?

VITALS treats 30 to 180 minutes of exercise a day as green and 15 to 240 minutes as the yellow watch range. On a real mission, the exercise plan comes from the flight surgeon and fitness team.

## Why does my back hurt in space?

In weightlessness the spine lengthens, and astronauts can grow up to a few centimetres taller. This stretching often causes back pain in the first weeks. Gentle stretches and pulling the knees to the chest can ease it. It's also why back injuries are a concern after landing.
