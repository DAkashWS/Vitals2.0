## What are the main dangers to astronaut health?

NASA groups the risks of human spaceflight into five hazards: space radiation, isolation and confinement, distance from Earth, gravity fields (weightlessness and changes in gravity), and hostile, closed environments. NASA's Human Research Program studies each of them to keep crews healthy on missions to the Moon and Mars.

## Radiation as a hazard

Outside Earth's protective magnetic field and atmosphere, astronauts are exposed to more radiation than on the ground. Over time this raises the risk of cancer and may affect the nervous system, heart and eyes. See the radiation section for more.

## Isolation and confinement as a hazard

Crews live and work in a small space with the same few people for months, far from family and friends. This can affect mood, sleep, teamwork and performance. Careful crew selection, routines, private time and contact with home help.

## Distance from Earth as a hazard

The farther a spacecraft is from Earth, the longer messages take and the harder it is to get help. A message to Mars can take up to about 20 minutes each way, and a return trip can take months. The crew must handle medical problems themselves, which is why on-board tools like VITALS matter.

## Gravity as a hazard

In weightlessness, body fluids shift toward the head, bones and muscles weaken, balance changes and the heart adapts. Moving between gravity levels (weightlessness, Moon, Mars, Earth) also affects coordination, balance and blood pressure.

## Hostile and closed environments as a hazard

The spacecraft is the crew's whole world. Air quality (CO2 levels), temperature, noise, lighting, microbes and limited space all affect health, so the life-support systems and cabin conditions are monitored closely.
